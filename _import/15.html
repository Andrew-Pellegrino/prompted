No American lab shipped a blockbuster model this week, and it barely mattered. The action was about who sets the terms of the race. China released the largest open-weight model ever published and, the same week, stood up a 29-country organization to promote its version of open AI. Europe stopped writing principles and started writing orders, telling Google exactly which Android doors to open for rival assistants. Anthropic went after the American classroom with a free product for K-12 teachers, and Google turned its research notebook into a workspace that writes and runs its own code. Even the safety news read like strategy, with OpenAI publishing the automated attacker it used to harden GPT-5.6 and DeepMind’s chief calling for a watchdog that could slow everyone down. Underneath all of it, the bills started arriving: protests against data centers in 42 states, White House planning to keep AI costs off electric bills, and investors openly asking how long the buildout can keep accelerating.

The Biggest Developments

China Turns Open Weights into Foreign Policy

On July 17, Moonshot, one of China’s leading AI startups, launched Kimi K3 and described it as a 2.8 trillion parameter open-source model built for long-horizon coding, reasoning, and knowledge work. Parameters are the numbers inside a model that store what it learned during training, so the count is a rough measure of capacity. By that measure, Reuters reports this is the largest open-weight model ever released, meaning the underlying model files are published for others to download and run. It reads images natively and carries a 1 million token context window, the working memory that sets how much material it can consider at once. Moonshot says it performs competitively with Anthropic’s frontier Fable line, and Reuters cited third-party evaluations from Arena.ai, Vals AI, and Artificial Analysis pointing to strong performance, though the boldest comparisons are still the company’s own. The full weights are due July 27. The release did not travel alone. The same week in Shanghai, 29 countries signed an agreement creating the World AI Cooperation Organization, and Xi Jinping used the World Artificial Intelligence Conference to pitch open-source AI as a “historic opportunity” and a public good for the developing world. Read together, it is a two-track strategy: cheap, capable open models that spread through developers, clouds, and enterprises, plus a diplomatic body that shapes whose standards become normal. The open questions are real, including how many organizations can actually run something this large and how Beijing squares its open-source pitch with its reported interest in restricting overseas access to some leading Chinese models.

Europe Orders Google to Open the Gates

On July 16, the European Commission issued binding specification measures to Google under the Digital Markets Act, the EU law that polices how the biggest platforms treat competitors. A specification measure is essentially a compliance order that spells out exactly what has to change. Google must open 11 Android features to rival AI assistants and share anonymized search-optimization data with qualifying AI and search competitors. The timeline has teeth: data sharing starts in January 2027, and the Android changes are meant to reach users in a future Android release from July 2027. Google argues the decisions could weaken privacy and security. This is the clearest statement yet from a major government that AI competition is not only about who has the best model. It is about invocation rights, background permissions, and data access, the plumbing that decides whether a rival assistant can wake by voice or act across apps on the phone in your pocket. Germany opened a second front the same week. Its media regulators said that Google’s AI Overviews and the AI search engine Perplexity fall under German media law, which treats their AI-written answers like published media rather than neutral search results. The trigger was court scrutiny of AI answers that got facts wrong. The practical meaning is simple: if the company counts as the publisher, the company owns the mistakes, and the legal exposure for AI search grows fast. Back on the EU order, the big unknowns are how much access rival companies will actually get in practice and whether Google’s appeals narrow it.

Anthropic Makes a Serious Play for the Classroom

On July 14, Anthropic launched Claude for Teachers, giving verified U.S. K-12 educators free access to premium Claude capabilities. The package is the story. It includes a library of teaching-specific skills, a Learning Commons connector tied to academic standards in all 50 states, dedicated teacher terms, a data agreement designed for compliance with FERPA, the federal law protecting student records, a commitment not to train models on Claude for Teachers data, and integrations with existing classroom tools rather than attempts to replace them. Anthropic also launched an AI fluency course for PK-12 teachers and plans an evaluation pilot in Detroit Public Schools Community District. Investors noticed: shares of Stride, an online-education company, fell on the announcement. The significance goes beyond education. Anthropic is treating teaching as a workflow and institutional-trust market rather than a student-user opportunity, and the package doubles as a template for selling AI into any profession where trust is the price of entry: purpose-built connectors, privacy guarantees, training, and evaluation plans bundled together. What remains unproven is everything that matters most, meaning adoption, district procurement reaction, and actual classroom impact.

Google Turns the Notebook into a Workbench

On July 16, Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook and shipped the change that actually matters: a secure cloud computer that lets notebooks write and execute code natively against your sources. In practice, that means a research notebook that does not just summarize the documents you load into it. It can run real analysis on them, and Google says the product will reach deeper into the Gemini app and eventually Search, with notebooks syncing across surfaces. Google says more than 30 million people and over 600,000 organizations have used it. The rollout starts with Google AI Ultra and certain Workspace customers, with Pro web users next. Separately, Google’s help pages show Gemini Spark, its persistent assistant that acts across Google’s tools, rolling out to U.S. Google AI Pro subscribers this week with more Workspace actions and smarter sourcing. The architectural read: Google is building the middle layer of AI knowledge work, not a generic chatbot and not a fully autonomous agent, but a grounded workspace that holds your sources, does real intermediate labor, and follows you across tools. Whether people prefer that hybrid format over pure chat or agent products is the open product question of the next six months.

Safety Becomes a Competitive Front

The week’s safety news shared a theme: the big labs are treating safety, testing, and governance as product strategy, not compliance overhead. The stories look scattered until you notice that each one is a different lab making that same argument in its own language. OpenAI made it with engineering. It published GPT-Red, an internal system that hunts for prompt-injection vulnerabilities, the attacks that hide malicious instructions inside text an AI reads, by training against itself with reinforcement learning until it finds what breaks. OpenAI says it used GPT-Red to adversarially train GPT-5.6 for higher robustness and claims it now beats humans on specific red-teaming tasks, results that remain the company’s own, and the system stays internal. OpenAI also made the argument with policy, publishing an essay arguing for what it calls reverse federalism, the idea that state AI safety laws should converge toward one national framework, and saying the Trump administration is working toward a federal cyber-testing framework by early August. Demis Hassabis made it with an institution, publicly calling for a U.S.-led AI watchdog modeled on FINRA, the self-regulatory body that polices American brokerages, with the power to screen frontier models and coordinate a slowdown if needed. Anthropic made it with measurement, publishing research that analyzed 309,815 conversations to track how Claude’s expressed values, along axes like caution, warmth, and candor, vary across models and languages, turning how a model behaves into something that can be audited. And Google DeepMind and Isomorphic Labs made it with biology, detailing a bioresilience program spanning more than 15 partnerships across outbreak prevention, detection, and response, a plan for one of the riskiest domains AI touches. Line them up and one thread runs through all of it: every lab is building public proof that its systems can be tested, measured, and controlled, because that proof is becoming part of what they sell. Some of it is genuine engineering, some is positioning for the regulatory fights now underway, and most of it is both. Either way, the message to governments and enterprise buyers is the same: model quality alone will not carry the next phase.

Worth Knowing Briefly

Compute is turning into a market of its own. Reuters reported that Meta is in early talks to lease computing power to Anthropic in a deal that could be worth up to $10 billion over two years, and separately that SpaceX is in talks to provide data-center capacity to the Pentagon for AI workloads. Neither is confirmed, and either could fall apart. They still matter as indicators: raw compute is being bought, sold, and leased like a strategic commodity, sometimes between direct competitors.

The buildout became a ratepayer issue and a protest movement in the same week. Reuters reported White House planning to convene utilities and data-center developers around an expanded voluntary pledge to keep AI-driven grid costs off households and small businesses. On July 18, opponents of data-center expansion held 142 separate protest events across 42 states in a single day, and earlier Reuters/Ipsos polling found only 14% of respondents would support an AI data center in their own community. The social license for AI infrastructure can no longer be assumed.

Investors started stress-testing the capex story. Reuters reported growing debate over whether hyperscaler AI spending, the tens of billions the biggest cloud companies pour into chips and data centers each quarter, can keep accelerating at the pace built into valuations, with UBS expecting growth to slow meaningfully after 2026. That is not a call that the buildout ends. It is the market starting to separate “AI demand is strong” from “AI capex can accelerate forever.” Alphabet’s earnings on July 22 are the first real test.

Sovereignty became an architecture decision. Airbus signed a multi-year deal with French cloud provider Scaleway to host sensitive industrial and defense applications supporting AI tools built with Mistral, explicitly citing legal requirements, data protection, and insulation from extraterritorial controls, meaning foreign governments reaching into your infrastructure through their own courts. It is one of the cleanest examples yet of AI sovereignty moving from conference talk into a signed contract.

xAI took a user to court. The company sued a Grok user accused of generating child sexual abuse material and explicit deepfakes, in what Reuters described as one of the first cases of an AI company suing a user over this kind of misuse. One lawsuit does not solve the deepfake problem, but it marks a shift: at least some labs are now willing to use litigation as part of their enforcement toolkit.

Patterns Worth Watching

Governance stopped being one government’s story and became a three-way race to write the rulebook. Two weeks ago, government-managed model access looked like an emergency posture. Last week it ran as routine process in Washington. This week it went structural, and multipolar. Europe issued binding orders reshaping who gets platform access and search data. China chartered a 29-country body to carry its open-source vision to the countries the U.S. and Europe are not courting. And in the U.S., the labs themselves started proposing referees, with Hassabis calling for a FINRA-style watchdog and OpenAI arguing state laws should fold into one national framework. Three blocs, three rulebooks, one week. The labs’ safety publications fit the same picture: GPT-Red, values measurement, and bioresilience programs are real engineering that also function as evidence files for the regulatory arguments now underway. The consequence is the one this newsletter keeps circling back to: who gets distribution, access, and trust is becoming as decisive as who has the best model.

China’s open-weights push has a contradiction at its center, and the contradiction is the strategy. Last week, Reuters reported that Beijing had discussed restricting overseas access to its top models. This week, Beijing pitched open-source AI to the Global South while its leading startup shipped the largest open-weight model ever released. That is not double-talk so much as a plan: give models away where giving them away wins friends and users, and hold them back where holding back protects an edge. There is also a practical catch underneath the rhetoric. A model this large is too big for almost any organization to download and run on its own machines, so most people will end up using it through a cloud service that someone else operates, and whoever runs that service still holds the on-off switch. Even open AI comes with a landlord. And one caveat before taking the pitch at face value: the actual model files are not public yet. Moonshot says they arrive on July 27, so within a week we will see whether the full version really ships or the open label comes with an asterisk.

The grounded-workspace thesis keeps winning, and trust just moved into the spec sheet. For three weeks running, the products that matter have wrapped AI around a specific environment rather than a chat box, and this week extended the streak with a notebook that executes code against your sources and a teaching assistant tied to state standards. What changed is the packaging. Claude for Teachers leads with a FERPA-designed data agreement, no-training commitments, and an evaluation pilot. Gemini Notebook leads with source grounding and a sealed cloud computer. The pitch is shifting from what the model can do to what an institution can trust it to do, and that shift favors whoever can prove reliability over whoever can claim capability.

The binding constraints on AI moved outside the model, and they are physical and political. In one week: compute leased like a commodity between rivals, sovereignty written into an Airbus contract, electricity prices elevated to White House planning, data-center siting turned into a 42-state protest movement, and the first serious investor questions about whether the spending curve can keep steepening. None of that shows up on a benchmark. All of it decides what actually gets deployed, where, and at what cost. The next phase of AI competition is a contest for permission, energy, capital, and trust, and it will be fought in utility commissions, county zoning meetings, and earnings calls at least as much as in training runs.

Why This Week Matters

If you are a student or future knowledge worker, the skill signal was unusually clear: learn to work inside source-grounded, tool-using AI environments, not just chat windows. Gemini Notebook now writes and runs code against the sources you give it, and Claude just became a colleague to your former teachers, with campus versions a plausible next step. The durable advantages are the ones these tools reward: evaluating sources, basic spreadsheet and code literacy, and the judgment to check what the machine hands back. A prompt trick is tied to one specific tool and dies when that tool changes, but those underlying skills are not tied to any single tool, so they carry over to whatever ships next.

If you are a founder or builder, two levers moved this week. The model menu widened at the open end, since Kimi K3 is a serious high-end option even if you consume it through an API rather than run it yourself, and more open competition tends to pull costs down. And the moats shifted: Android invocation rights, search-data access, sovereign hosting, and safety posture all became contested terrain in a single week, which means distribution and compliance now deserve as much strategic attention as model choice. Keep fallbacks across model families, and if you touch Europe, the AI Act’s broader application date lands August 2.

If you are faculty, the education story finally arrived from the vendor side. Claude for Teachers signals where classroom AI is heading: teacher workflow support, standards alignment, and institutional privacy guarantees, not student shortcuts. It is K-12 today, and higher-ed packaging is the obvious next move. The productive response likely stays what it has been, meaning AI literacy, process evidence, and judgment-based assessment design rather than a bet on prohibition. Gemini Notebook sharpens the point: students now have a research environment that reads sources and runs analysis, which changes what “show your work” has to mean.

If you are staff, the workspaces are arriving inside tools your organization already licenses. Gemini Notebook’s cloud computer does source-grounded analysis without a data team, and Spark is spreading through Workspace with more actions and smarter sourcing. The read from recent weeks holds: the advantage goes to whoever can set up, verify, and maintain these workflows for a team. Note the vendor behavior too. Anthropic’s teacher package is a preview of how AI will be sold to institutions from here on, guarantees first, features second.

If you are an administrator, three developments carry institutional weight. AI built for specific fields now arrives ready for institutional buyers, and Claude for Teachers sets a concrete bar for what to demand from any AI vendor: a data agreement built around student-privacy law, a commitment not to train on your data, and a real evaluation pilot. The rules of access are being written in real time on three continents, which strengthens the case for multi-vendor flexibility and against long exclusive commitments. And the infrastructure politics, from ratepayer costs to local protests to investor scrutiny, are now material to any decision that touches data centers, energy contracts, or long-horizon computing budgets.
