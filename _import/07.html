This week didn’t deliver one blockbuster model. It delivered a direction. Across Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Nvidia, the same shift kept surfacing: AI is moving from something you chat with to something you hand work to, systems that plan, act, run in the background, and connect to real tools. Google built its entire I/O keynote around it. Anthropic showed what it looks like aimed at cybersecurity. OpenAI claimed a real math result. And Nvidia’s earnings were a reminder that all of it still runs on an enormous, expensive stack of hardware. The takeaway for the week: “using AI” increasingly means supervising it, not prompting it.

The Biggest Developments

Google Made Its Whole Stack Agent-First

On May 19, Google I/O laid out one of the clearest “agents everywhere” roadmaps a major platform company has put on the table. Google introduced Gemini 3.5 (starting with a faster, cheaper variant called 3.5 Flash), made that model the default in Search’s AI Mode, added an AI-powered search box and “information agents,” and expanded agents that can book and shop on your behalf. For developers, it previewed Managed Agents and an Antigravity Agent that can plan a task, write and run code, manage files, and browse the web inside a secured sandbox.

The point isn’t the model refresh. It’s that Google pushed agents into search, its assistant app, developer tools, and enterprise deployment all at once, trying to make “delegate the task” a native part of mainstream computing rather than an opt-in feature. Google also said AI Mode passed one billion monthly users, though that’s Google’s own number, not an independent count. The real test is whether the agents hold up in daily use, or stay impressive mostly on stage.

AI Finds Security Holes Faster Than Anyone Can Patch Them

On May 22, Anthropic published its first real update on Project Glasswing, its cybersecurity effort. The claim is striking: Anthropic and roughly 50 partners say they’ve found more than ten thousand high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in important software. Using an unreleased model it calls Mythos Preview, Anthropic scanned over 1,000 open-source projects and says the model flagged thousands of serious issues, and where those have been checked so far, about 90% held up as real.

Here’s the part that matters even if you don’t care about security: the bottleneck is shifting. Finding bugs used to be the hard part. Now the hard part is triaging, disclosing, and actually shipping fixes. Anthropic said some maintainers asked it to slow disclosures down because they can’t patch fast enough. The real risk window is the stretch where systems can be found and broken faster than institutions can repair them. Anthropic also says it hasn’t released Mythos-class models publicly because its safeguards aren’t strong enough yet. Keep in mind these are Anthropic’s own figures.

An OpenAI Model Reportedly Cracked a Real Math Problem

On May 20, OpenAI said one of its general reasoning models disproved a longstanding conjecture in discrete geometry, a real open problem rather than a textbook exercise. OpenAI added that outside mathematicians checked the proof. It stressed this wasn’t a narrow, math-only system but a general-purpose model, and described the result as the first time AI autonomously solved a problem central to a math subfield.

If it holds up, the interesting part isn’t the geometry. It’s the signal that frontier models may be starting to do original research in areas where answers can be rigorously checked. The mathematicians quoted said the proof connected basic geometry to deeper algebra in a way they found genuinely surprising, not just a remix of known techniques. The open question is reproducibility: how often models can do this outside carefully chosen problems, and how much hidden compute it took.

The Compute Race Got Bigger and Changed Shape

Nvidia reported record quarterly revenue of $81.6 billion, up 85% from a year earlier, with $75.2 billion of that from data centers, and authorized another $80 billion in share buybacks. Separately, Blackstone and Google announced a U.S.-based “TPU cloud” venture, with Blackstone putting in an initial $5 billion to bring 500 megawatts of capacity online in 2027 while Google supplies the chips and software.

Nvidia’s numbers show the buildout is still enormous. The Blackstone deal shows the market is also mutating: compute is becoming its own product to sell, not just a cost buried behind the models, and private-capital firms are now wiring themselves into the AI supply chain. For anyone building, compute price and availability increasingly shape product design. Whether you go cloud-only, hybrid, or self-hosted is now a real strategic decision, not a footnote.

U.S. AI Policy Tilted Toward Exports Over Oversight

The policy signal this week was mixed but telling. Reuters reported the White House briefed leading AI firms on a planned executive order that would have frontier developers notify the government before major releases and potentially share advanced models with agencies up to 90 days early. Then, on May 21, Trump postponed the signing, reportedly because he didn’t want it to slow the U.S. lead over China. Meanwhile, the U.S. Export-Import Bank launched ExportAI to finance exports of American AI technology, and a bipartisan group of senators introduced a bill to help allied countries buy U.S. AI rather than Chinese alternatives.

The throughline: U.S. AI policy is increasingly about export power and strategic competition, not abstract safety principles. The government seems to want two things at once: closer pre-release engagement on frontier models, and faster global deployment of U.S. AI to stay ahead. That tension will likely shape policy for the rest of the year.

Worth Knowing Briefly

Enterprise AI moved deeper into core systems. Anthropic announced an alliance to embed Claude across KPMG and its 276,000+ employees; OpenAI partnered with Dell to bring its Codex coding tool into on-prem and hybrid setups. A Thomson Reuters survey found 40% of professionals say their organization uses generative AI, up from 22% a year ago, but only 18% track its return on investment. Adoption is outrunning measurement.

Provenance tools got real. OpenAI became a “C2PA conforming generator” (an industry standard for tagging where media came from), added DeepMind’s SynthID watermarking to ChatGPT images, and previewed a tool to verify whether an image came from OpenAI. Google expanded similar verification across Search, Chrome, and Pixel. Both admitted no single watermark survives screenshots and reposting, so provenance is becoming layered infrastructure, not one magic label.

DeepSeek made a 75% price cut permanent. China’s DeepSeek locked in a steep, permanent discount on its flagship V4-Pro model, dropping API prices to a quarter of their earlier level. Expect continued pricing pressure, especially on high-end models.

Google leaned into AI for science. It launched Gemini for Science, a bundle of research tools for hypothesis generation and literature analysis, and extended its Project Genie world-simulation work by grounding generated environments in Street View imagery.

Courts are tightening up on AI. A California jury ruled for OpenAI in Elon Musk’s lawsuit, finding it was filed too late. And a federal judge in Alabama suspended a lawyer after a filing contained fake AI-generated quotations, and after he deleted his ChatGPT account when records were requested.

Patterns Worth Watching

Agents are leaving the chat box. Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI all pushed AI toward planning, using tools, and acting over time rather than just answering well.

The capability frontier is splitting into many frontiers. This week’s strongest results were in math, cybersecurity, and scientific tooling, not another chatbot benchmark. Progress now shows up in specific domains, each with its own rules for what counts as “correct” and its own risk profile.

Trust and control are becoming product features. Watermarking, provenance, security tooling, and government review talks all point the same way: the contest is no longer just “who has the best model” but “who can deploy powerful systems without breaking user trust, compliance, or security.”

Compute and geopolitics are tightening into one story. Chips, infrastructure financing, API pricing, and national alignment are now part of the same competition.

Why This Week Matters

If you’re a student or future knowledge worker: the skill appreciating fastest isn’t prompting. It’s task design, verification, and judgment. The systems pulling ahead work over longer horizons and act with partial autonomy, which means the valuable human is the one who can define constraints clearly, check outputs against real sources, and recognize when something needs a second look. Being “good with AI” is starting to look a lot like being a strong editor and operator.

If you’re a founder or builder: design for agent supervision, deployment flexibility, and cost realism. Google is exposing more agent building blocks, OpenAI is pushing coding agents into governed environments, and DeepSeek’s cuts mean the market is getting more price-sensitive. Build in approval steps, observability, and the ability to switch vendors or move between cloud and self-hosted setups, and don’t assume the model pricing your margins are built on will hold.

If you’re an educator: this is the week to take assessment design seriously. The strongest signals all landed at once: research-grade reasoning, coding agents, classroom-scale deployment. Assignments that capture process, sources, iteration, and reasoning under constraints will hold up better than ones that just ask for a finished answer. Google and OpenAI are both pushing teacher training and school deployments, so faculty judgment will matter most where institutions choose to use AI thoughtfully rather than ban it.

If you create or share content: start treating provenance as a normal part of your workflow. Both OpenAI and Google moved toward watermarking and verification this week, and both admitted no single signal is bulletproof. Know what your tools attach to your outputs, preserve metadata where you can, and treat “where did this come from?” as a basic literacy question, for what you make and what you share.
