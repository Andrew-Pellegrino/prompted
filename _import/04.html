This was a structural week for AI, not a flashy one. The biggest stories weren’t about which model felt smartest, they were about who controls distribution, who pays for compute, and who’s quietly integrating frontier models into critical systems. OpenAI loosened its grip on Microsoft and showed up on AWS. Hyperscaler earnings put combined 2026 AI capex at roughly $700 billion. The Pentagon onboarded seven labs onto classified networks. China’s domestic AI stack, model, chip, cloud, kept tightening. And U.S. cyber officials started seriously weighing shorter patch deadlines because AI is making attackers faster. Less spectacle, more infrastructure.

The Biggest Developments

Big Tech’s AI Capex Hit a New Gear

Alphabet’s Google Cloud grew 63% year-over-year to $20B in Q1, its fastest reported growth since the segment was broken out, and Reuters reported Google has started selling its TPU chips directly to outside customers. Microsoft’s Azure-and-related-cloud revenue rose 40%, and its AI business hit a $37B annual run rate. AWS grew 28% to $37.6B and reaffirmed a $200B AI investment target. Meta raised its 2026 capex forecast to $125–$145B. Stack those four together and the implied combined 2026 capital spending is roughly $695–$725 billion.

Why it matters. The AI revenue case is no longer hypothetical, it’s showing up in actual cloud growth, but the cost of staying competitive is enormous. That tension will shape startup pricing, chip and power demand, hiring, and how patient public markets stay through the rest of the year. Sundar Pichai called enterprise AI Google Cloud’s primary growth driver for the first time. Whether returns keep outrunning the cost curve is the open question.

OpenAI Loosened Its Microsoft Deal, and Showed Up on AWS

On April 27, OpenAI amended its Microsoft partnership. Microsoft remains the primary cloud partner and OpenAI products will still ship first on Azure unless Microsoft can’t or won’t support the required capabilities. But the Microsoft license is now non-exclusive through 2032, and OpenAI is explicitly free to serve all its products across any cloud. One day later, OpenAI announced GPT-5.5, Codex, and Bedrock Managed Agents in limited preview on AWS, with OpenAI saying more than 4 million people now use Codex weekly.

Why it matters. This isn’t a breakup, it’s a normalization. Frontier-model distribution is becoming multi-cloud rather than vertically captive. The leading model lab can keep its flagship cloud relationship while still competing for every major enterprise buying path. For developers and enterprises with big AWS commitments, OpenAI is now reachable without re-architecting around Azure first.

The Pentagon Went Wide on AI Vendors

DOD reached agreements with seven companies, SpaceX, OpenAI, Google, NVIDIA, Reflection, Microsoft, and AWS, to deploy advanced AI on classified networks. Reuters reported the Pentagon’s GenAI.mil platform has already been used by more than 1.3 million Defense personnel after five months, and that onboarding to secret and top-secret levels has accelerated since the Anthropic dispute. Reuters also reported, citing The Information, that Google’s classified deal includes language barring use for domestic mass surveillance and autonomous weapons without human oversight, though the contract isn’t fully public.

Why it matters. This is a clear shift from “defense interest” to defense infrastructure, more vendors, faster integration, deeper access. It also sharpens the question every frontier lab now faces: how much military use will you permit, and where exactly is the line.

DeepSeek’s V4 Fallout: Price Cuts and a Huawei Chip Scramble

Following up on last week’s V4 release, Reuters reported DeepSeek offered developers a 75% discount on V4-Pro through May 5 and cut input-cache-hit prices to one-tenth of prior levels across its API. Because V4 is optimized for Huawei hardware, the launch also triggered a scramble among major Chinese internet companies to secure Huawei Ascend 950-series chips. Quick recap: V4 Preview itself dropped April 24, open-sourced, with a 1M-token context window, a more powerful Pro model, and a cheaper Flash variant.

Why it matters. The story this week isn’t the model itself, it’s the second-order effects: a tightening domestic stack where model, chip, cloud, pricing, and industrial policy are aligned. Export controls alone look like a less reliable bottleneck than they did a year ago.

AI Is Becoming a Timing Problem in Cybersecurity

Reuters reported U.S. officials are weighing cuts to the deadline for fixing critical government-system vulnerabilities, from roughly two-to-three weeks down to three days, because advanced AI tools can find and exploit weaknesses much faster. Australia’s prudential regulator warned banks that frontier systems like Anthropic’s Mythos could enable larger and faster cyberattacks, and said boards still lack the technical literacy to oversee them well. China’s cyberspace regulator separately launched a four-month campaign against AI misuse, including weak security review, data poisoning, unlabeled AI content, and impersonation.

Why it matters. Defenders are losing reaction time, and patch cycles, oversight models, and regulation now have to update faster than institutions are used to. AI security is moving from a model-governance issue to an operational-security one.

The Musk–OpenAI Trial Opened

Elon Musk’s lawsuit against OpenAI over its for-profit restructuring kicked off in federal court in Oakland. Musk is seeking $150 billion in damages, leadership changes, and a return to nonprofit status. Reuters reported he testified he didn’t read the “fine print” of a 2017 term sheet related to the for-profit structure. OpenAI argued that commercial restructuring was necessary to raise capital and that the suit is driven by Musk’s loss of control.

Why it matters. The interesting layer is bigger than founder drama. OpenAI’s legal structure feeds fundraising, governance, future public-market plans, the Microsoft relationship, and the broader credibility of nonprofit-to-commercial AI governance models. Even an inconclusive verdict adds uncertainty to one of the sector’s most important firms.

Worth Knowing Briefly

Gemini file generation. Google rolled out file generation in the Gemini app, Docs, Sheets, Slides, PDFs, Word, Excel, CSV, LaTeX, text, RTF, and Markdown, directly from a prompt.

NotebookLM in Google Classroom. Google Workspace began rolling out personal class notebooks with NotebookLM for higher-ed students 18 and older, grounded study guides, flashcards, audio overviews, video overviews, diagrams, infographics, and slide decks built from course materials.

OpenAI Advanced Account Security. Opt-in mode that requires passkeys or physical security keys, disables password login, shortens sessions, and excludes those conversations from training.

South Africa pulled its draft AI policy. After fake AI-generated citations were found in its references. Useful warning that polished AI text can carry fabricated evidence.

EU AI rule talks stalled. Reuters reported negotiators failed to reach a deal on a watered-down rules package and will resume later.

J\&J on AI in pharma. Said AI has halved the time it takes to generate drug-development leads and reduced one regulatory-document process from hundreds of hours to minutes.

Meta–Manus unwind. China ordered Meta to unwind its $2B+ acquisition of Manus, signaling rising cross-border AI deal risk.

New U.S. AI bills. A chatbot-family-accounts proposal and a broader bill supporting AI research, standards, education, and cybersecurity training.

Patterns Worth Watching

Distribution is the new strategic edge. OpenAI’s amended Microsoft deal and Google selling TPUs directly point to a market where placement across many enterprise buying paths beats a single captive distribution pact.

Stronger commercial story, harder capital story. Hyperscalers are showing real top-line lift from AI, but they’re also training customers and investors to expect very large ongoing infrastructure bills. That favors firms with deep balance sheets and integrated stacks.

Security and sovereignty are converging with mainstream adoption. The Pentagon push, the cyber-policy reaction, and China’s model-plus-chip alignment all treat frontier AI as strategic national capability, not just software.

AI literacy is now an institutional problem, not just a personal one. NotebookLM and easy file generation lower the bar for individual use, while South Africa’s pulled policy shows what happens when institutions skip verification.

Why This Week Matters

If you’re a student or future knowledge worker. The cost of organizing material, generating study aids, and producing polished documents keeps falling. Your edge moves toward judgment, source selection, fact-checking, domain knowledge, and being able to defend your reasoning. The South Africa episode is a useful warning: polished AI text can still carry fabricated evidence.

If you’re a founder or builder. Don’t confuse model access with durable advantage. OpenAI’s distribution changes mean more flexibility on where you buy frontier capability, but the hyperscaler earnings make clear that infra economics, security posture, cloud commitments, and enterprise procurement fit are becoming as important as raw model quality. As the model layer commoditizes, product, workflow, data, and compliance strategy matter more.

If you’re an educator. Course-specific AI assistance is moving toward default software. NotebookLM-backed class notebooks plus easy file generation lower the effort of producing study materials and polished assignments. That likely raises the importance of designing work that rewards process, oral defense, reflection, source handling, and class-specific application, not formatting.

If you’re an operator or security-minded. The line between AI adoption and AI security is collapsing. With AI moving into coding, research, document systems, and classified environments, account security, vulnerability response, model-governance literacy, and vendor concentration risk become board-level questions sooner than most organizations planned.
